How does it work?

  • Algae (diatoms) multiply with light and the nutrients in the water; they decline when the light is dimmed or the nutrients run out.
  • Bacteria multiply on the nutrients added to the water and on the remains of dead organisms; they are decomposers.
  • Euglena photosynthesises in light and gets by on bacteria in the dark.
  • Paramecium eats algae and bacteria, splits in two when well fed and dies if it starves.
  • Didinium hunts only paramecia; if it finds no prey it starves.
  • Viruses infect any paramecium or Didinium they bump into; a few seconds later the cell bursts and new viruses spill out. Viruses decay over time.
  • Temperature speeds up the metabolism of every organism: they eat more, multiply faster and get hungry sooner.

Gause's experiment

In 1934 the Russian biologist Georgy Gause put paramecia (Paramecium caudatum) and their predator Didinium into small tubes. With nowhere to hide, Didinium ate every paramecium and then starved itself: the predator–prey relationship did not sustain itself. When he added sediment at the bottom of the tube where the prey could hide, Didinium still died out, but the hidden paramecia multiplied again. Only when he added new individuals to the tubes at regular intervals (immigrants) did the two populations rise and fall in waves that followed each other. The experiment was one of the first laboratory demonstrations that a predator–prey balance in nature can last only with refuges and migration.
