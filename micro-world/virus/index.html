How does it live?

A virus is basically genetic material (DNA or RNA) packed inside a protein coat. Bacteriophages (phages), the viruses that infect bacteria, often look like a lunar lander: a geometric head, a tail and leg fibres.

The phage recognises the right bacterium with its tail fibres, latches onto its surface and injects its genetic material into the cell like a syringe. The cell drops its own work and makes hundreds of new viruses, finally bursting to release them.

Fun facts

  • There are an estimated 1031 phages on Earth: far more than the number of stars in the universe.
  • Marine viruses are thought to kill a large share (perhaps 20–40%) of the ocean's bacteria every day; the released nutrients go back into the plankton.
  • Phage therapy, which uses phages as medicine against antibiotic-resistant infections, is being researched again.

Game vs. reality

  • In the game: In the game you can shake viruses off with a dash.
    In reality: In reality once a virus is inside a cell, there is no escape except the cell's own defences (or systems like CRISPR in bacteria).
  • In the game: In the game small cells can eat viruses.
    In reality: This is real: some single cells (such as the ciliate Halteria) can eat viruses as food; this was shown in 2022.
  • In the game: In the game organisms collect the genes of what they eat.
    In reality: In reality genes are mostly passed from parent to offspring. Close parallels to getting genes from food are rare: horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, and a cell turning a swallowed alga into an organelle (endosymbiosis; this is how mitochondria and chloroplasts arose).
  • In the game: In the game you pick evolution cards while alive.
    In reality: In reality an individual does not evolve during its life; mutations build up from generation to generation. The game's generation inheritance is therefore closer to reality.
