Paramecium · Paramecium caudatum

Erdal Üstünol · Nov 26, 2026 · Micro World Encyclopedia

A slipper-shaped single cell that swims by beating thousands of cilia like oars and sweeps bacteria into its oral groove.

How does it live?

The surface of a paramecium is covered with thousands of cilia that beat in regular waves, like oars. While pushing the cell forward, the cilia also drive water into the oral groove on the side of the body; bacteria are carried by this current to the cell mouth and packed into food vacuoles.

When it bumps into an obstacle it reverses its cilia, backs off, turns a little and swims forward again. This simple "bump, back up, turn" behaviour is a classic example of how a single cell explores its surroundings.

The cell has two kinds of nucleus: a large macronucleus that runs daily life and a small micronucleus used in reproduction. When two individuals line up side by side and swap pieces of their micronuclei, it is called conjugation, a primitive form of sexual reproduction.

Fun facts

  • When threatened, it fires tiny harpoon-like darts called trichocysts from its surface.
  • Its natural enemy is Didinium; the rise and fall of these two populations was the subject of G. F. Gause's famous predator–prey experiments in the 1930s.
  • Its shape, rounded at the front and pointed at the back, earned it the nickname "slipper animalcule".
  • It is one of the easiest organisms to see in a school microscope: just soak a handful of hay or a lettuce leaf in water for a few days.

    In reality: In reality genes are mostly passed from parent to offspring. Close parallels to getting genes from food are rare: horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, and a cell turning a swallowed alga into an organelle (endosymbiosis; this is how mitochondria and chloroplasts arose).

    In reality: In reality an individual does not evolve during its life; mutations build up from generation to generation. The game's generation inheritance is therefore closer to reality.
