Life in a drop of water

Grades 4–6 (ages 9–12) · 40 min (one lesson)
MS-LS1-1 Provide evidence that living things are made of cells: either one cell or many different numbers and types of cells
MS-LS1-2 Model the function of a cell as a whole and how its parts contribute to that function

Materials: Interactive whiteboard or projector · A phone, tablet or computer per student (or per group) · If available, a school microscope and some pond water or hay infusion that has stood for a few days · Observation sheet (printable, at the end of this page)

5 minSpark curiosityShow a drop of water magnified 100 times on the board (the Micro World home page). Ask "How many living things are in this drop?" and collect guesses.
10 minEncyclopedia tourGive each group one organism: amoeba, paramecium, euglena, diatom, tardigrade. Each group notes 3 facts from the ID card: size, feeding and movement.
10 minObserve through playPlay Organizma for 8–10 minutes. Task: "Write down 3 different microorganisms you saw and how they move on your observation sheet." You can leave the screensaver mode running on the class board.
10 minClass quizMake a class code in the panel below and have students answer the same 10 questions. Results appear live in the panel; discuss the question most students got wrong.
5 minWrap-upWhy is Euglena called "half plant, half animal"? Compare plant and animal cells through the cell wall, chloroplasts and movement.

Discussion questions

  1. What might microorganisms have in common that sets them apart from plants and animals?
  2. How does Euglena feed in light and in the dark? Does that make it more like a plant cell or an animal cell?
  3. Why can an amoeba keep changing shape? (Hint: cell membrane and cell wall)

From cell to organism: colonies and multicellularity

Grades 6–7 · 40 min
MS-LS1-3 Argue from evidence for how the body is a system of interacting subsystems composed of groups of cells

Materials: Projector · Student devices · The Volvox page

5 minQuestion"A single cell does every job by itself. So how do we, made of billions of cells, work?"
10 minStudy VolvoxRead the Volvox page: a colony of thousands of cells swimming together, where some cells only reproduce. Write "division of labour" on the board.
12 minForm a colony in the gameIn Organizma, grow big enough to divide (a new generation), then press 🔗 Form colony next to your offspring. The colony protects you from being swallowed but slows you down. Students note the gains and losses.
10 minConcept mapDraw the levels cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism, comparing Volvox (a colony) with a human. At which level does a colony stop?
3 minExit ticketA one-sentence answer to "What is the difference between a colony and a multicellular organism?"

Discussion questions

  1. Is Volvox one organism or a community of cells living together? Give your reasons.
  2. What were the advantages and disadvantages of forming a colony in the game? What might they correspond to in real organisms?

Biodiversity and the factors that threaten it

Grades 6–8 · 40 min
MS-LS2-4 Argue from empirical evidence that changes to physical or biological components of an ecosystem affect populations

Materials: Student devices · The Micro World Lab

5 minIntroductionWhat would happen if only one species were left in a lake? Write the predictions on the board.
15 minLab experimentOpen the "Balanced pond" preset in the lab and watch for 2 minutes. Then each group changes one variable: switch off the light, set the nutrient flow to zero, raise the temperature or add viruses. Each group notes from the graph which species declined and which increased.
10 minThe estuary in the gameIn Organizma, travel between fresh water (the middle) and the sea (the outer ring). Why does a freshwater organism shrink in salt water? Watch the Salinity and Oxygen rows in the life panel.
10 minConclusionMake a table from the groups' data: threat → organism affected → outcome. Discuss what pollution, warming and habitat loss mean for real lakes.

Discussion questions

  1. Which change reduced diversity the most in the lab? Explain with your data.
  2. If warm waste water flowed into a lake, how would oxygen and the organisms be affected?

Food chains, decomposers and the predator–prey balance

Grades 7–8 · 40 min
MS-LS2-1 Analyze data on the effects of resource availability on organisms and populations
MS-LS2-2 Explain patterns of interactions among organisms across ecosystems
MS-LS2-3 Model the cycling of matter and flow of energy among living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem

Materials: Student devices · The lab · The Didinium page

5 minBuild the chainDraw the chain algae → paramecium → Didinium on the board. Mark the producer, the consumers and the decomposer.
15 minGause's experimentIn the lab, run the presets "Gause 1934 · no escape", "Gause · with refuge" and "Gause · with immigrants" in turn. Compare the prey and predator population curves: why do both die out in one, while in another they live on in waves?
10 minDecomposersIn Organizma, a brown cloud of decay and a bacterial biofilm form where cells die, and oxygen drops there. Discuss their place in the food web and the cycling of matter.
10 minEcological pyramidUse the game's population numbers (plankton, microbes, cells, Didinium) to draw a pyramid. Why do the levels get narrower towards the top?

Discussion questions

  1. Why did adding a refuge in Gause's experiment let the two species live together?
  2. What would happen in a lake without decomposers?

Seasons, mutation and adaptation

Grade 8 · 40 min
MS-ESS1-1 Model the Earth–sun system to describe the cyclic pattern of the seasons
MS-LS3-1 Explain why mutations may result in harmful, beneficial or neutral effects
MS-LS4-4 Explain how genetic variations of traits increase some individuals' probability of surviving and reproducing

Materials: Student devices · The game Organizma

5 minIntroductionLook at the 🍂 Season row in the game's life panel: a year in the game lasts 1 hour and each season 15 minutes (the same clock for everyone). As in a real lake, the water cools to about 6 °C by the end of winter and warms to about 24 °C by the end of summer. In the real world this difference is caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis and the angle of the sun's rays.
10 minTemperature and lifeThe Q10 rule: a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the metabolic rate. In the game, compare the Metabolism row in the deep (cold) water and near the warm vents. Talk about the spring algal bloom.
15 minMutation and natural selectionMove on a generation in the game: each offspring is born with one random mutation (most small, some harmful). The 🧬 Natural selection table in the family tree shows how long siblings lived depending on their mutation. Pool the class's data.
10 minDiscussionDoes a mutation happen inside one individual, or does it spread over generations? Is adaptation something an individual "wants"? Answer with data from the game.

Discussion questions

  1. In the game, did siblings with beneficial mutations really live longer? What does your data show?
  2. Why do single-celled organisms form cysts when the water gets cold in winter?
