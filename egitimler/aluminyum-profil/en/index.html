Aluminium Profiles and Fabrication

A diagram-rich 7-day programme on façade and window profiles from material to production: alloy and temper selection, extrusion, profile design rules, anodizing and powder coating, thermal breaks, cutting and joints in the workshop, and cutting optimisation. At the end you can read and prepare a profile order, a finish specification and a cutting list correctly.

7 days~4 hours a dayEnglishCode examples can be copied

Before you start

Who is it for

Design, fabrication and purchasing teams of façade and window companies, system designers and architects. For background see Facade Design Fundamentals (day 2: profiles).

What you need

  • A calculator.
  • A system supplier's profile catalogue (section area, weight per metre, second moments of area) makes the work easier.

DAY 01

Material and alloys

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know why aluminium is used in façades, the 6000-series alloys, temper (heat treatment condition) codes and the basic mechanical properties. Time about 4 hours.

About this course: It explains the production chain of façade profiles with diagrams, from material and extrusion to surface finishing and cutting and joining in the workshop. Values are typical and approximate; actual values come from the relevant standards (EN 755, EN 573, EN 1999) and the manufacturer's documents.

1. Why aluminium? (45 min)

Property Value (approx.) Meaning in façades
Density 2.7 g/cm³ (~1/3 of steel) Light profiles, easy installation
Modulus of elasticity E 70 000 N/mm² (~1/3 of steel) Three times the deflection of steel for the same section: profiles are deep
Thermal expansion α 23 × 10⁻⁶ /K (~2× steel) Joints and sliding connections needed
Thermal conductivity ~200 W/mK Thermal break essential (day 5)
Corrosion Natural oxide layer Long life with surface finishing (day 4)
Forming Very suitable for extrusion Complex multi-cavity sections in one piece

2. Alloys (1.5 hours)

Façade profiles are 6000-series (Al-Mg-Si) alloys: easy to extrude, heat-treatable, suitable for surface finishing.

Alloy Use Yield strength Rp0.2 (T6/T66, approx.)
EN AW-6060 The most common architectural alloy; very good surface quality, finely detailed profiles 150-160 MPa
EN AW-6063 Slightly stronger than 6060 170-200 MPa
EN AW-6005A Structural profiles, mullions ~215 MPa
EN AW-6082 High strength, brackets, thick sections ~250 MPa

Selection is a balance between strength and surface quality: 6082 is stronger but after anodizing does not look as even as 6060.

3. Temper codes (1.5 hours)

The same alloy has different strength depending on its heat treatment condition. The code is written after the alloy name: 6060-T66.

Code Meaning
T4 Solution treated, naturally aged (soft; for bending)
T5 Controlled cooling from extrusion temperature, artificially aged
T6 Solution treated and artificially aged (full hardness)
T66 Higher-strength T6 variant for 6060/6063

Artificial ageing: Profiles are held in an oven at around 180 °C for hours; magnesium silicide precipitates increase strength. An unaged profile is soft and will not carry the calculated load. The alloy and temper in the structural calculation must be the same in the order and the certificate.

DAY 02

Extrusion

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know the extrusion process step by step from billet to profile, be able to distinguish die types and calculate a profile's weight per metre. Time about 4 hours.

1. Extrusion process (1.5 hours)

  1. Billet: A cast aluminium cylinder heated to ~400-500 °C.
  2. Press: A hydraulic press pushes the billet through a steel die; the aluminium takes the die's shape and comes out as a profile (like a toothpaste tube).
  3. Quench: The profile is cooled quickly with air or water; important for T5/T6 strength.
  4. Stretching: The profile is straightened by stretching it 0.5-3% of its length; internal stresses are reduced.
  5. Cutting: Cut to sales lengths (e.g. 6000-7000 mm).
  6. Ageing: Artificial ageing in the oven (day 1).

The press capacity (tonnes) and the circumscribing circle diameter that fits the die limit the largest profile section that can be produced.

2. Die types (1 hour)

Profile Die Note
Solid Flat die Cheapest and fastest
Semi-hollow Flat die, tongue Long narrow tongues tend to break
Hollow Porthole die The aluminium is split by bridges and re-welds inside the die; weld seams may show after anodizing

Die cost depends on the complexity of the profile; a new system design needs dozens of dies. Extruders usually require a minimum order quantity (e.g. a certain tonnage per die).

3. Weight per metre (1 hour)

Weight per metre (kg/m) = Section area (cm²) × 0.27

Example: section area 12 cm² → 12 × 0.27 = 3.24 kg/m
         6.5 m bar → 21.1 kg

1 cm² × 100 cm × 2.7 g/cm³ = 270 g = 0.27 kg. Profile catalogues give weight per metre and section area together. Weight is the basis of both material cost (price per kg) and transport (the Façade Quantity Take-off course).

End-of-day check (15 min)

Exercise: How many kg is a 3.6 m piece of a mullion with a section area of 18.5 cm²? (Answer: 18.5 × 0.27 × 3.6 ≈ 18.0 kg.)

DAY 03

Profile design

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know the design rules for a good extrusion profile, details such as screw ports and visible faces, profile tolerances and what the section properties (A, I, W) mean. Time about 4 hours.

1. Design rules (1.5 hours)

Rule Why
Wall thicknesses as even as possible Aluminium flows fast where thin and slowly where thick; the profile bends and marks remain on the surface
A radius at sharp inner corners Die life and flow; less stress concentration
Avoid long thin tongues Risk of breaking in the die (tongue ratio)
Symmetry Balanced flow, straight profile
The section must fit within the circumscribing circle Press and die limit
Minimum wall thickness (e.g. 1.2-1.5 mm) Depends on profile size and alloy

2. Functional details (1 hour)

  1. Screw port: A C-shaped channel that self-tapping screws grip. Its size is standard for the screw diameter; the mouth is left slightly open.
  2. Gasket grooves: Shaped to the gasket; tight tolerances.
  3. Visible face mark: Visible faces are marked on the drawing; extrusion and finishing take care of the quality of those faces.
  4. Guide lines and grooves: Small marks showing hole and cut positions in the workshop.

3. Tolerances (45 min)

Standard Scope
TS EN 755-9 Dimensional and shape tolerances for general extruded profiles
TS EN 12020-2 Tighter tolerances for 6060/6063 precision profiles (façade and window systems)

Tolerances are given separately for section dimensions, wall thickness, straightness, twist, angle and length. Dimensions where gaskets and accessories must fit exactly are marked separately on the drawing.

4. Section properties (45 min)

Property Meaning Used in
A (cm²) Section area Weight, axial force
Ix, Iy (cm⁴) Second moment of area Deflection (Building Physics of Façades, day 6)
Wx, Wy (cm³) Section modulus Bending stress
Centroid The balance point of the section Axis and bracket alignment

Because wind acts perpendicular to the façade, the second moment of area along the mullion's depth governs; it grows with the cube of the depth. The Profile Section tool in the site's custom tools calculates these values for a drawn section.

DAY 04

Surface finishes

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know how anodizing and powder coating are done, the thickness classes, quality labels and what to watch for when choosing colours. Time about 4 hours.

1. Anodizing (1.5 hours)

Anodizing electrochemically thickens aluminium's natural oxide layer. It is not a coating but a conversion of the surface.

  1. Pretreatment: Degreasing, etching (matt look) or polishing.
  2. Anodic oxidation: The oxide layer grows with direct current in a sulphuric acid bath.
  3. Colouring (optional): Electrolytic colouring (bronze, black shades) or dye.
  4. Sealing: The pores in the oxide layer are closed; essential for durability.
Thickness class Use (typical)
AA15 Interior, mild exterior
AA20 External façade (general)
AA25 Seaside, industrial environment

Standard: EN ISO 7599; quality label: QUALANOD. In anodizing, alloy and extrusion differences can show in the colour shade; on the same façade, profiles from the same batch and approval of colour samples are important.

2. Powder coating (1.5 hours)

  1. Pretreatment: Degreasing, etching and a conversion coating (mostly chrome-free today). Paint adhesion and corrosion resistance depend on this step.
  2. Coating: Powder is applied with an electrostatic gun.
  3. Curing: In the oven at ~180-200 °C the powder melts and hardens.
Topic Typical value
Thickness At least ~60 µm (on visible surfaces)
Durability class QUALICOAT Class 1 (standard), Class 2 and 3 (super durable, more resistant to sun and UV)
Quality label QUALICOAT, GSB

Other coatings: PVDF-based liquid paint (very high colour retention), wood-effect sublimation. Colour is given as a RAL code; gloss (matt, semi-matt, gloss) and texture (fine texture) are specified separately. See the colours in the site's RAL Colour Chart.

3. Points to watch in finishing (45 min)

  1. Jig marks: Profiles hang on jigs during coating and anodizing; jig marks must fall on a non-visible face or an end that will be cut off.
  2. Thermally broken profiles: The polyamide must withstand the powder coating oven temperature; profiles are often coated before the thermal break is fitted (different colours inside and outside are possible).
  3. Cut ends: Cut ends are uncoated; if visible they are covered.
  4. Damage and repair: Touch-up paint on site never matches the colour exactly; protective film and careful handling are the best prevention.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 05

Thermal break

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know the construction of thermally broken profiles, how polyamide strips are assembled, the shear strength of the thermal break and its effect on the structural behaviour of the profile. Time about 4 hours.

1. Why a thermal break? (45 min)

Aluminium conducts heat very well (~200 W/mK). A one-piece profile becomes a bridge carrying indoor heat outside; the inner surface gets cold and condensation starts (Building Physics of Façades, days 2 and 4). The solution is to split the profile into inner and outer parts and join them with a low-conductivity material.

2. Polyamide thermal break (1.5 hours)

  1. Material: Glass-fibre reinforced polyamide (PA66 GF25), thermal conductivity ~0.3 W/mK; about 1/700 of aluminium.
  2. Assembly: The grooves of the inner and outer profiles are knurled, the polyamide strips slid in, then the groove edges are crimped with rollers to lock the strips tightly.
  3. Shear strength: The resistance of the thermal break to slip along the profile is tested (EN 14024). If the inner and outer parts are to work together under wind, this bond must be sound.
  4. Shape: From simple flat strips to multi-chamber shapes forming a second cavity filled with foam.

3. Pour-and-debridge resin (45 min)

Liquid polyurethane is poured into a channel inside the profile; once hardened, the aluminium bridge beneath is milled away (debridging). Simple and economical, but the break width is limited.

4. Structural behaviour (1 hour)

How much the inner and outer parts of a thermally broken profile work together depends on the shear stiffness of the polyamide:

Assumption Stiffness
Fully bonded (parts act as one section) Highest; not reached in reality
Unbonded (parts act separately) Lowest; on the safe side
Partially bonded (with EN 14024 test values) Realistic; given by the system supplier

In curtain walls the structural member is usually the inner mullion and the calculation is often done with the inner profile only; the outer part (pressure plate, cover cap) is not counted as structural. In windows and doors, however, both parts are included and the shear bond matters.

Temperature difference: The difference in expansion between a sun-heated outer part and a cool inner part tries to bend the thermally broken profile (bimetal effect). In long, dark-coloured profiles this can cause vents to open and sealing problems.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 06

Workshop fabrication

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know the workshop production steps of a façade element, the cutting, machining and punching machines, corner and T joints and how sealing is achieved in the workshop. Time about 4 hours.

1. Workshop flow (1 hour)

Step Machine / tool Output
Cutting Double-head mitre saw Length and angle (90°, 45°, special)
Machining CNC machining centre Holes, pockets, end milling, drainage slots
Punching Press and special dies Repeated holes and notches (fast, repetitive)
End milling End milling machine Transom ends shaped to fit the mullion
Assembly Corner crimping press, screwing, bonding Frame, panel
Sealing Sealant, gasket Water and air paths sealed
Inspection Dimensional and visual check Label and item no.

The fabrication drawing (Technical Drawing, day 6) is the input to every step: cutting list, machining drawings, joint details.

2. Cutting (1 hour)

  1. Mitre cut (45°): For frame corners; the angles of the two parts add up to 90°.
  2. Square cut (90°): Mullions, transoms, T joints.
  3. Special angles: Raked corners; the angle comes from the true angle in the Descriptive Geometry course (day 4), not the plan angle.
  4. Cut length and saw kerf: The cutting list must state clearly whether the mitre length is given on the long or the short edge; this confusion is the most common fabrication error.

3. Joints (1.5 hours)

Joining How Use
Crimped corner A corner cleat is inserted into the profile and the corner press crimps the profile wall inwards Vent and window frames
Pinned corner The corner cleat is fixed with pins Thick profiles
Bonded + mechanical Corner cleat with two-part adhesive, plus crimping or pins Sealed and rigid corner
T joint (transom-mullion) Connector (bridge) in the transom end, screwed to the mullion Stick curtain wall
Screwed (unitized frame) Transom ends screwed to the mullion Modular panel frame

Sealing: Sealant is applied to the cut surfaces of corner and T joints before joining, or a sealing gasket (corner seal) is fitted. This is the workshop counterpart of the second sealing stage (Facade Design Fundamentals, day 5).

DAY 07

Cutting optimisation

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

On the last day you learn cutting optimisation: cutting the same parts from fewer stock lengths, calculating scrap and managing offcuts. Then you sum up the week in a production checklist. Time about 4 hours.

1. What is cutting optimisation? (45 min)

Profiles come in 6000-7000 mm lengths. A project cuts hundreds of parts of different lengths. Deciding which parts are cut from which stock length is a one-dimensional cutting problem. A good plan directly reduces scrap and therefore cost.

Input Example
Stock profile length 6500 mm
Saw kerf 4 mm (lost at each cut)
End trim 10 mm at each end (damaged ends)
Parts list Length and quantity

2. Worked example (2 hours)

Parts: 10 × 2150, 6 × 1450, 8 × 980, 4 × 620 mm. Total part length 40 520 mm.

Simple way (one part type per length):

2150 → 3 pieces per bar → 4 bars
1450 → 4 pieces          → 2 bars
 980 → 6 pieces          → 2 bars
 620 → 10 pieces         → 1 bar
Total 9 bars = 58 500 mm → scrap ≈ 30.7%

Placing from the largest (first fit decreasing): Parts are sorted from largest to smallest and each is placed in the first length it fits.

Bar 1-3 : 2150 + 2150 + 2150                 offcut 18
Bar 4   : 2150 + 1450 + 1450 + 980           offcut 434
Bar 5   : 1450 + 1450 + 1450 + 1450 + 620    offcut 40
Bar 6   : 980 × 6                            offcut 576
Bar 7   : 980 + 620 + 620 + 620              offcut 3624
Total 7 bars = 45 500 mm → scrap ≈ 10.9%

Two fewer stock lengths: 22% less aluminium for the same job. And if the 3624 mm offcut of the last length can be used for another job, real scrap is even lower.

3. Offcut management and tools (45 min)

  1. Usable offcut limit: Offcuts above a certain length (e.g. 1000 mm) are labelled and shelved; shorter ones are scrapped.
  2. Use offcuts first: Offcuts in stock are also entered into the optimisation program.
  3. Profile type and colour: Optimisation is done separately for each profile code and colour.
  4. Tools: The Profile Optimisation (bars) and Sheet Optimisation (sheet and composite panel, two-dimensional) tools in the site's custom tools section do this calculation.

4. Production checklist (15 min)

Step Inspection
Material Alloy, temper, certificate
Profile Die no., tolerances, visible face
Finish Colour, thickness, QUALICOAT/QUALANOD, sample approval
Thermal break Shear test values
Cutting List, mitres, true angles, optimisation
Machining and joining Drawings, sealant, corner check
Dispatch Label, item no., protection, installation sequence

End-of-day check (15 min)
