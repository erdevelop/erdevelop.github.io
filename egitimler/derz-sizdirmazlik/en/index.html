Joints and Sealing

Most leaks in façades come not from glass or profiles but from joints and connections. In this diagram-rich 7-day programme you learn which forces drive water in, how joint width is calculated, how to choose sealants and gaskets, the logic of structural silicone design, the membranes between building and façade, and how application is checked on site.

Before you start

Who is it for

Façade designers, project and site engineers, quality control teams. Prior knowledge: Facade Design Fundamentals.

What you need

  • No prerequisites; a calculator is enough for the calculations.
  • If you have a project detail, you can use it in the exercises.

DAY 01

How does water get in?

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know how water and air get into a façade, the idea of two lines of defence, pressure equalisation and drainage. Time about 4 hours.

About this course: Most leaks in façades come not from glass or profiles but from joints and connections. This course covers sealants, gaskets, structural silicone and membranes, how joint movement is calculated and how application is checked. It complements the Building Physics of Façades and Mock-up and Performance Testing courses (in Turkish). Numerical examples are for teaching; material selection and calculation are done with manufacturer data and the relevant standards.

1. Which forces drive water in? (1.5 hours)

For water to pass through an opening, three things are needed at once: water, an opening and a force driving the water in. The forces:

Force Example Measure
Gravity A surface sloping inwards Outward slope, drip
Kinetic energy A drop driven by wind Closed or labyrinth joint
Surface tension Water creeps inwards along the underside of a surface Drip (drip nose)
Capillarity Water is drawn into a narrow gap Open the gap (> about 5 mm) or close it fully
Air currents Wind carries air and water Air barrier
Pressure difference Outside pressure higher than inside Pressure equalisation

It is impossible to reduce openings to zero; a good detail eliminates the forces.

2. Two lines of defence (1 hour)

  1. Outer line (rain screen): Keeps most of the water outside. Outer gasket, outer sealant, cladding panel.
  2. Inner line (air barrier): Airtight seal. Inner gasket, inner sealant, membrane.
  3. The cavity between: The small amount of water passing the outer line collects here and is expelled through drainage holes.

Rule: Air seal inside, rain screen outside. If the inner line is airtight, wind pressure is held at the inner line and does not drive water through openings in the outer line.

3. Pressure equalisation (45 min)

If small vent openings are left in the outer line, the cavity pressure approaches the outside pressure. The pressure difference across the outer line becomes small; the force driving water in is reduced. The full wind pressure is carried by the inner line (air barrier).

For this to work:

  1. The inner line must be truly airtight.
  2. The cavity must be divided into compartments at corners and at intervals (wind pressure varies along the façade).
  3. Vent and drainage openings must be sufficient and must not clog.

4. Drainage (30 min)

  1. Water inside the transom (horizontal profile) is expelled through drainage holes; the holes are usually baffled or labyrinthine.
  2. At the mullion–transom joint water is directed outside, not from the transom into the mullion.
  3. Holes must not be blocked during installation or painting; this is checked in the mock-up test.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 02

Joint movement

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to calculate how much a joint will move: thermal expansion, building movements and tolerances; and find the required joint width from the sealant's movement capability. Time about 4 hours.

1. Why do joints move? (45 min)

Cause Example
Thermal expansion An aluminium panel heated by the sun expands
Storey drift Floors move relative to each other in wind and earthquakes
Slab deflection Edge beam deflection increases under load
Shrinkage and creep Concrete shortens over time
Tolerances Fabrication and installation differences (not movement, but they change the joint width)

2. Thermal movement (1 hour)

ΔL = α × L × ΔT

α  aluminium ≈ 23 × 10⁻⁶ 1/K   (steel ≈ 12, glass ≈ 9, concrete ≈ 10)
L  panel length = 3000 mm
ΔT surface temperature range = 80 K   (e.g. −20 °C to +60 °C; more for dark colours)

ΔL = 23 × 10⁻⁶ × 3000 × 80 ≈ 5.5 mm

The joint between two adjacent panels takes the sum of half the elongations of the two panels; in the end it again accommodates an expansion equal to one panel length. The temperature range is chosen by colour and orientation; it is wider for dark colours and south façades.

3. Movement capability of the sealant (1 hour)

Sealants can extend and compress by a certain percentage of the width at which they were applied. EN ISO 11600 classes: 25 (±25%), 20 (±20%), 12.5 and 7.5. Some silicones reach ±50%.

Required width:   w ≥ movement / movement capability

If the sealant is applied at the mean temperature, movement is ±ΔL/2:
  w ≥ (5.5 / 2) / 0.25 ≈ 11 mm

If the application temperature is unknown (worst case: at one extreme):
  w ≥ 5.5 / 0.25 ≈ 22 mm

Fabrication and installation tolerance is added to this: if a joint drawn at 15 mm ends up 10 mm on site, the movement capability is calculated on that 10 mm.

Conclusion: Joint width is not an "appearance choice" but a calculated value. Narrow joints are the most common cause of sealant tearing.

4. Storey drift and slab deflection (45 min)

  1. Storey drift: Comes from the structural calculation (a ratio of storey height). Stack joints and corner joints are designed for this movement.
  2. Slab deflection: The vertical joint of the stack joint must accommodate the relative deflection of the slabs below and above.
  3. These values are obtained in writing from the structural engineer and entered in the design criteria document (Façade Installation).

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 03

Sealant

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know sealant types and where they are used, the correct joint geometry (width, depth, backer rod) and why three-sided adhesion must be avoided. Time about 4 hours.

1. Sealant types (1.5 hours)

Type Strong Weak In façades
Silicone (neutral) UV and weather resistance, wide temperature range, high movement Cannot be painted; may stain natural stone (choose a non-staining grade) Weatherseal joints, around glass, structural silicone
Polyurethane (PU) Paintable, abrasion resistant Degrades in UV over time Joints in concrete and precast panels
MS polymer (hybrid) Paintable, good adhesion, primerless on most surfaces Movement capability varies by type General-purpose external joints
Butyl Very low vapour permeability, does not cure Not structural, low movement Primary seal of insulating glass, membrane tapes
Polysulphide Fuel and chemical resistance Lower UV resistance than silicone Secondary seal of insulating glass (non-structural)

Acetic (vinegar-smelling) silicone can corrode metals and some surfaces; neutral-cure silicone is used in façades.

2. Joint geometry (1 hour)

  1. Width / depth: For elastic sealants the depth is usually about half the width (e.g. 12 mm wide → 6 mm deep); the minimum depth and ratios come from the manufacturer's table.
  2. Hourglass section: The sealant is thin in the middle and wide at the edges where it bonds; when it extends, stress does not concentrate at the bond.
  3. Backer rod: Closed-cell polyethylene; its diameter is chosen about 25% larger than the joint width (12 mm joint → 15 mm rod), it stays in place by compression and sets the depth.
  4. Bond-breaker tape: Used when the joint is too shallow for a rod, to stop the sealant bonding to the base.

3. Three-sided adhesion (45 min)

The sealant must bond only to two opposite surfaces. If it also bonds to the base (three sides), it cannot extend freely when the joint opens and tears from the corner at the base. A backer rod or bond-breaker tape prevents this.

4. Compatibility (30 min)

  1. The sealant must be compatible with gaskets, paint, the insulating glass edge seal and other sealants. Incompatible materials can cause discolouration, loss of adhesion or leaks in insulating glass.
  2. The manufacturer carries out compatibility and adhesion tests on submitted material samples and issues a written report; this report is part of the approval process (Façade Project Management, day 4).

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 04

Gasket

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know façade gaskets: EPDM and other materials, compression, corner joints, outer and inner gaskets, dry and wet sealing, shrinkage. Time about 4 hours.

1. Gasket materials (45 min)

Material Property
EPDM Most common in façades; good UV, ozone and temperature resistance; vulcanized corners possible
Silicone gasket Very wide temperature range, compatible with structural silicone; more expensive
TPE Can be heat-welded; easy to recycle
Sponge (cellular) EPDM Low compression force; for vent gaskets

Hardness is given in Shore A; façade gaskets are usually around 60-70 Shore A.

2. Compression (1 hour)

A gasket seals only when it is compressed.

  1. Too little compression: the gasket does not seat fully on the surface; water and air pass.
  2. Too much compression: glazing becomes difficult, the gasket takes a permanent set, too much force reaches the glass edge.
  3. The system supplier gives a table of which gasket to use for each glass thickness. If the glass thickness changes, so does the gasket.

Compression set: The inability of a gasket compressed for a long time to return to its original shape. Low compression set means long life.

3. Corners and shrinkage (1 hour)

  1. Corner: The weakest point for water. Options: a vulcanized (moulded) corner frame, bonding at the corner or completing with sealant.
  2. Shrinkage: Gaskets can shorten over time after installation; gaps open at corners. Measure: fit the gasket not stretched but slightly over-length (e.g. a few mm per metre) and compressed; bond the corners.
  3. Splice: A splice on a straight edge is bonded; splices must not coincide with corners or drainage holes.

4. Dry and wet sealing (45 min)

Dry (gasket) Wet (sealant)
Application In the factory or during installation, little affected by weather On site, affected by temperature and humidity
Replacement Easy Cut out and reapply
Movement Limited Calculated capability
Appearance Black line Colour can be chosen

Many systems use both: gasket outside, gasket inside and sealant at critical joints.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 05

Structural silicone

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know structural sealant glazing (SSG): load transfer, the logic of the silicone bite calculation, glass weight and setting blocks, the secondary seal of insulating glass and quality requirements. Time about 4 hours.

1. What is structural silicone? (45 min)

In structural sealant glazing the glass is not held by an external cover cap but bonded to the supporting frame with silicone. From outside a flush glass surface is seen.

  1. Wind load passes from the glass to the silicone and from the silicone to the frame.
  2. In Europe design and test rules are defined by ETAG 002 (EAD) and EN 13022.
  3. Some countries and regulations require additional mechanical retention (against glass falling) above a certain height.

2. Silicone bite (1.5 hours)

The width over which the silicone bonds at the glass edge must be enough to carry the wind load. Basic calculation logic (ETAG 002):

h_c = a × W / (2 × σ_des)

a      short edge of the glass     = 1500 mm
W      design wind load            = 1.8 kN/m² = 0.0018 N/mm²   (1.2 kN/m² × 1.5)
σ_des  design stress of silicone   = 0.14 N/mm² (short-term load, ETAG 002)

h_c = 1500 × 0.0018 / (2 × 0.14) ≈ 9.6 mm  → 10 mm

Had the same glass been calculated with 1.2 kN/m², the result would be 6.4 mm: skipping the load factor means undersizing the silicone by about one third.

Rules:

  1. According to ETAG 002, bite and silicone thickness are at least 6 mm.
  2. Silicone thickness (between glass and frame) is calculated from the shear movement due to the different thermal expansion of glass and aluminium.
  3. The final calculation and approval are made by the silicone manufacturer in a project-specific check.

3. Glass weight (45 min)

Silicone can carry permanent loads only at a much lower stress (in ETAG 002 about 0.01 N/mm² instead of 0.14 N/mm² for short-term loads). This is why in most systems the weight of the glass is given not to the silicone but to the setting blocks at the bottom edge and the profile carrying them. The setting block material must be compatible with the silicone.

4. Insulating glass and quality (45 min)

  1. In a structural silicone system the outer pane of the insulating glass is also carried by silicone; therefore the secondary seal of the insulating glass must also be structural silicone and must be calculated. Polysulphide or PU secondary seals are not suitable here.
  2. Bonding is mostly done in the factory in a controlled environment: cleaning, primer, temperature, humidity and mixing ratio (for two-part silicone) are recorded.
  3. For each batch a butterfly test (mix homogeneity) and pull/peel tests (adhesion) are carried out and recorded.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 06

Membranes and interfaces

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know the sealing details where the façade meets the building: membranes, slab edge, parapet and sill, lap direction, the position of the vapour barrier and the fire interface. Time about 4 hours.

1. Membranes (1 hour)

Gaps between the façade and the building (slab edge, column, wall) are closed not with the façade system's own gaskets but with membranes.

Membrane Location Property
EPDM membrane Between façade and slab/wall Flexible, accommodates movement; bonded or sealed
Vapour barrier (inside) Warm (inner) side of the thermal insulation Vapour-tight or almost
Breather membrane (outside) Cold (outer) side of the thermal insulation Keeps water out, lets vapour through

Rule: The inner side must be less vapour permeable than the outer side. Otherwise vapour is trapped inside the construction and condenses (Building Physics of Façades).

2. Lap direction (45 min)

Membranes are lapped like roof tiles, the upper over the lower; water runs out of the lap, not in. Lap width follows the manufacturer (usually 50-100 mm). A reversed lap turns the membrane itself into a water channel.

3. Critical interfaces (1.5 hours)

  1. Slab edge: Between façade and slab, air and water sealing (membrane) and fire sealing (stone wool and fire-rated sealant) are made as separate layers without compromising each other (Fire and Regulations).
  2. Parapet and roof interface: The roof membrane is turned up behind the façade; the parapet top is closed with an outward-sloping coping.
  3. Sill: Ends turned up (stop ends), sloping outwards, with a drip; joints lapped and gasketed. A sill with open ends discharges water straight into the wall.
  4. Ground interface: A water check, drainage and membrane between the façade bottom transom and the ground; the external ground level must be lower than the bottom of the façade.
  5. Interfaces between systems: Curtain wall and cladding, window and wall. Responsibility limits (who installs which membrane?) must be written clearly on the drawing.

4. Responsibility and drawings (30 min)

Most leaks at interfaces come from work that falls between two contractors. The detail drawing states who installs each layer and in which order; intermediate inspections (photos before covering) are scheduled.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 07

Application and inspection

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

On the last day you will learn sealant application and quality control: surface preparation, primer, weather conditions, application and tooling, site adhesion test, records, maintenance and a final exercise. Time about 4 hours.

1. Surface preparation (1 hour)

Most sealant problems are loss of adhesion, and most loss of adhesion comes from dirty surfaces.

  1. Two-cloth method: Wipe with one cloth and the solvent recommended by the manufacturer, then dry with a second dry cloth before the solvent evaporates. Wiping with one cloth only spreads the dirt.
  2. Primer: If the manufacturer requires it, apply after cleaning and within the stated time; wait for the primer's drying time.
  3. The surface must be dry: rain, dew and frost spoil adhesion.

2. Weather conditions (30 min)

Condition Rule (typical, varies by manufacturer)
Temperature About +5 °C to +40 °C
Surface moisture Surface temperature at least a few degrees above the dew point
Rain None during and immediately after application

Joint width varies with the temperature at the time of application (day 2): sealant applied on a very hot or very cold day uses up most of its movement capability in one direction.

3. Application (45 min)

  1. Place the backer rod at the correct depth (day 3).
  2. Masking tape along the edges.
  3. Apply the sealant by pushing the gun, leaving no air voids.
  4. Tool the surface with a spatula: the sealant is pressed against both surfaces, forming the hourglass section.
  5. Remove the tape before the sealant cures.

4. Inspection and records (1 hour)

  1. Site adhesion test: A short strip is cut in the cured sealant and pulled by hand at 90°. The sealant should tear within itself (cohesive failure); if it peels cleanly from the surface (adhesive failure), adhesion is insufficient. Test location, frequency and recording are agreed with the manufacturer.
  2. Water test: Critical joints are checked with a hose test (Mock-up and Performance Testing).
  3. Records: Date, weather, applicator, material batch number, test result.

Maintenance: Sealants and gaskets have a shorter life than the building. The maintenance manual given to the building owner must include periodic inspection and renewal.

5. Final exercise (30 min + your own time)

  1. Calculate the thermal movement and required joint width for a panel joint in one of your projects.
  2. Choose the sealant type, backer rod diameter and depth.
  3. Do the bite calculation for a structurally glazed pane.
  4. Draw the layer sequence of the slab-edge interface (membrane, fire, vapour barrier) in a section and write the responsibilities.

End-of-day check (15 min)
