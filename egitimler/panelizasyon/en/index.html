Panelisation and Rationalisation

A diagram-rich 7-day programme on turning curved and free-form façades into producible panels and making them economical. From curvature and planarity analysis to surface families that give flat panels, from cold bending to type reduction with tolerances, from substructure to fabrication data. Numerical examples were calculated with Python.

7 days~4 hours a dayEnglishCode examples can be copied

Before you start

Who is it for

Façade designers, computational design and Grasshopper users, architects working on free-form projects. Background: Rhino and Grasshopper basics.

What you need

  • Rhino 8 and Grasshopper; Kangaroo2 for planarisation (comes with Rhino 8).
  • A free-form surface from your own project makes the exercises meaningful.

DAY 01

Form and panels

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know why a free-form façade is expensive, the surface types (flat, single-curved, double-curved), the cost order of panel types and the concept of "rationalisation". Time about 4 hours.

About this course: It teaches turning curved and free-form façades into producible panels (panelisation) and making this economical (rationalisation). The examples use Rhino and Grasshopper; the numerical examples were calculated and checked with Python. Knowing surface division and data trees in Grasshopper is enough background.

1. Architectural form, fabrication reality (45 min)

The architect draws a surface; the façade is built from a finite number of parts made of real materials. Closing the gap is the job of panelisation:

  1. Divide the surface into panels.
  2. Check that each panel is actually producible (is it flat, can it be bent?).
  3. Reduce the panels to repeating types (moulds, cutting, ease of installation).
  4. Solve the geometry of the substructure and joints.
  5. Generate the fabrication data (dimensions, codes, DXF).

2. Surface types (1.5 hours)

Type Gaussian curvature Example Panel condition
Flat 0 Flat façade Flat panel
Single-curved (developable) 0 Cylinder, cone Obtained by bending a flat sheet
Double-curved, synclastic (dome) > 0 Sphere, dome Cannot be obtained by bending; needs stretching or a mould
Double-curved, anticlastic (saddle) < 0 Hyperbolic paraboloid Cannot be obtained by bending; twisted

Gaussian curvature is the product of the two principal curvatures at a point. If it is zero the surface can be made from a piece of plane without tearing or stretching (a developable surface). You can roll paper into a cylinder but not into a sphere.

3. Cost order of panel types (1 hour)

From cheap to expensive:

  1. Flat panel: Standard production; glass, composite, sheet metal, stone.
  2. Cold-bent flat panel: Produced flat and slightly twisted during installation or in its frame. It has limits (day 4).
  3. Single-curved bent panel: Hot-bent glass, cylindrically bent sheet; needs a mould or machine setting.
  4. Double-curved panel: A mould for each different panel; the most expensive route in glass and metal.

The cost of a façade is set not only by panel type but also by the number of different panels: if 400 of 400 panels are different, each means a separate drawing, cutting, code and installation tracking.

4. Rationalisation (30 min)

Rationalisation is making the form follow production rules while preserving the architectural intent as much as possible. There are two approaches:

  1. Rational from the start: Build the form with producible geometries (surface of revolution, translational surface, developable surface; day 3). The cheapest and cleanest route, but it needs a decision at the start of the design.
  2. Rationalised afterwards: Take the free surface, planarise it, reduce the number of types, use tolerances (days 4 and 5).

In real projects both are used together. The biggest gain is having the façade engineer at the table in the early stage of design.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 02

Analysis

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to analyse a surface's curvature in Rhino and Grasshopper, calculate the planarity deviation of a quadrilateral panel and produce a deviation map. Time about 4 hours.

1. Curvature analysis (1 hour)

  1. Rhino: the CurvatureAnalysis command; Gaussian and mean curvature colour maps. On the Gaussian map, areas near zero are developable, positive areas dome-like and negative areas saddle-like.
  2. Zebra (Zebra): shows the smoothness of the surface and continuity problems.
  3. Grasshopper: Surface Curvature (Gaussian and mean curvature, principal directions); Evaluate Surface.

2. Planarity deviation (1.5 hours)

The three corners of a triangle are always in a plane. The four corners of a quadrilateral usually are not. Two ways to measure:

  1. Warp: The distance of the fourth corner from the plane formed by the other three corners.
  2. Deviation from the best-fit plane: The largest distance to the best-fit (least squares) plane through the four corners. In a warped quadrilateral the corners lie alternately above and below this plane and deviation = warp / 4.

Example: One corner of a 1500 × 1500 mm square panel is 20 mm up. Warp is 20 mm; deviation from the best-fit plane is ± 5 mm.

In Grasshopper: the Planar component (Surface → Analysis) tells whether a surface is planar and its deviation. To calculate it yourself from the corner points, use Python in the Rhino 8 Script component:

# Input: P (List Access, 4 points)   Output: d (mm)
import Rhino.Geometry as rg

ok, plane = rg.Plane.FitPlaneToPoints(P)
d = max(abs(plane.DistanceTo(p)) for p in P)

3. Deviation map (1 hour)

Calculate each panel's deviation and colour the panel by its deviation:

Colour Deviation Meaning (example thresholds; set per project)
Green < 1 mm Flat panel
Yellow 1-5 mm Cold bending or tolerance in the joint
Red > 5 mm Bent panel or form change

Example: a 30 × 15 m surface, 200 panels of 1.5 × 1.5 m

Surface Maximum deviation > 1 mm > 5 mm
Translational surface (different arcs in two directions) 0 mm 0 panels 0 panels
Hyperbolic paraboloid (z = x·y / 40 m) 14.1 mm 200 panels 200 panels
A "bump" surface rising 1.5 m in the middle 18.1 mm 184 panels 116 panels

Why the first row is zero is the subject of day 3.

4. Exercise (15 min)

Divide one of your own surfaces with a 1.5 m grid, produce the deviation map and note the share of red panels.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 03

Division strategies

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know strategies for dividing a surface into panels: UV division, cutting with planes, triangles, planar quad meshes, surfaces of revolution and translation, and planarisation. Time about 4 hours.

1. Division methods (1 hour)

Method How Pros / cons
UV division The surface's parameter lines Easy; but panel sizes are unequal and edges may not align with floor lines
Cutting with planes Horizontal floor planes + vertical grid planes Edges align with floors and grids; transoms and mullions become planar curves
Equal arc length Equal spacing along a curve Equal panel widths; helps type reduction

On façades cutting with planes is usually preferred: mullions stay in vertical planes and transoms in floor planes; this greatly simplifies the substructure.

2. Triangles (45 min)

Triangles are always flat; that is why any free surface can be covered with triangles. The price:

  1. Twice as many panels as quads on the same surface and a longer total joint length.
  2. 6 members meet at nodes (4 for quads); the node detail gets complicated.
  3. Unusable corner area and cutting waste increase in triangular glass.
  4. A visually "faceted" surface.

The example surface with 200 quad panels becomes 400 panels with triangles.

3. Planar quads: magic surfaces (1.5 hours)

Some surface families give perfectly flat quads when divided with the right grid:

  1. Surfaces of revolution: Rotating a curve about an axis (dome, tower). Divided along meridians and parallels, every quad is flat (an isosceles trapezoid). Example: a latitude-longitude quad on a sphere of 20 m radius, deviation 0 mm. On the same sphere, a similar-sized quad lying diagonally to the latitude-longitude lines has a deviation of 15.8 mm.
  2. Translational surfaces: Created by sliding one curve along another (z = f(x) + g(y)). Divided with a grid in the translation directions, opposite edges of the quads are parallel and the panel is flat. This is the zero-deviation example from day 2.
  3. Scale-translational surfaces and conjugate curve networks: More general families; advanced (Kangaroo and special plug-ins).

Lesson: If the form is built from one of these families at the start of the design, flat glass and flat panels come for free.

4. Planarisation (30 min)

To make the quad mesh of a free surface planar, you move the corner points slightly with the Planarize goal in Kangaroo2, together with surface proximity (OnMesh or OnSurface) and edge length goals. Result: flat panels, but the surface changes slightly. How much deviation is allowed must be discussed with the architect.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 04

Absorbing the deviation

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know ways to rescue non-planar panels: cold bending, joints and tolerance, single-curved bending, panel thickness and offset problems. Time about 4 hours.

1. Cold bending (1.5 hours)

A panel produced flat is twisted during installation by pushing one corner. Advantage: flat panel price, curved appearance.

Material Cold bending
Monolithic glass (toughened, laminated) Possible; the limit depends on glass thickness, size and stress
Insulating glass More limited: the edge seal is strained, the two panes deform differently
Aluminium sheet and composite panel Usually easy; thin sheet twists readily
Stone, ceramic Not suitable

Important: A permanent stress is created in cold-bent glass; it must be calculated together with wind and temperature loads. There is no single number for acceptable warp: glass type, thickness, panel size, the insulating glass edge seal and the frame detail decide. The project needs a finite element calculation and the glass manufacturer's approval (Glass Design).

Deviation and warp: On day 2 we calculated deviation relative to the best-fit plane; in cold bending the corner displacement applied to the panel is the warp itself: warp = 4 × deviation. A 14 mm deviation means a 56 mm warp.

2. Joints and tolerance (1 hour)

Small deviations can also be absorbed without bending:

  1. Joint width: Panels stay flat, a small step forms between neighbouring panels and the joint hides it. The visual acceptance limit is set with the architect.
  2. Adjustable fixings: Bracket and panel fixings give separate height adjustment at each corner.
  3. Cladding or glass? In ventilated cladding, open joints and an adjustable substructure easily tolerate deviations; with glass the tolerance is much tighter.

3. Single-curved bending (45 min)

If deviations are large, the panel can be produced cylindrical (single-curved): hot-bent glass or bent sheet. A double-curved surface can be approximately covered strip by strip with developable panels; the strip direction is chosen close to one of the surface's principal curvature directions.

4. Thickness and offset (30 min)

A panel has a thickness and a substructure behind it. On a curved surface:

  1. On the inner surface, which is an offset of the outer one, the panels have different sizes (on a dome the inside gets smaller).
  2. The side faces of mullions meet neighbouring panels at different angles; the profile is either machined to this angle or a gasket/spacer taking up the angle is used.
  3. For members to meet on the same axis at nodes (a torsion-free node), mullion directions are defined like the average of the neighbouring panel normals.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 05

Type reduction

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will learn to reduce the number of different panels (types): measuring panel similarity, grouping with tolerance, the tolerance-type count relation and negotiating between design and number of types. Time about 4 hours.

1. Why the number of types? (30 min)

Each different panel type means a separate drawing, cutting program, code, storage location, installation check and spare. For bent panels each type means a separate mould. Reducing the number of types often saves more than reducing deviation.

2. How is panel similarity measured? (1 hour)

A flat quadrilateral panel is fully defined by four edge lengths and two diagonal lengths (without the diagonals the quadrilateral can "skew"). These six numbers are the panel's signature. If the largest difference between two panels' signatures is smaller than the tolerance, they are the same type: the larger one is cut and used in place of the smaller, or the difference stays in the joint.

3. Grouping with tolerance (1.5 hours)

The simplest method is greedy grouping:

def count_types(signatures, tol):
    representatives = []
    for e in signatures:
        if not any(max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(e, r)) <= tol for r in representatives):
            representatives.append(e)          # new type
    return len(representatives)

Example: the "bump" surface from day 2, 200 panels

Tolerance Number of types
1 mm 186
3 mm 134
5 mm 80
10 mm 23

A 5 mm tolerance reduces 200 panels to 80 types, 10 mm to 23 types. The visual and technical price of the tolerance (difference in joint width, step at panel edges) is assessed jointly by the architect and the façade team.

Symmetry gives free types: Because the example translational surface is symmetric, 200 panels reduce to 72 types with 5 mm tolerance; a much better starting point than asymmetric forms.

Methods giving better results: k-means clustering, "pulling" panels towards types and updating the surface accordingly (with Kangaroo). In Grasshopper the Sort and Group components or the function above in a Script component can be used.

4. Negotiating with design (45 min)

Lever Effect
Pull the form towards a rational family (day 3) Zero deviation, number of types reduced by symmetry
Reduce panel size Deviation falls (roughly with the square), panel and joint counts rise
Increase tolerance Number of types falls quickly; price in joints and appearance
Let edge panels be special Repetitive interior, special edges

DAY 06

Substructure

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to build the geometry of the substructure behind the panels: transom and mullion curves, profile orientation, nodes, connection points and adjustment allowance. Time about 4 hours.

1. From curves to profiles (1 hour)

  1. Planar curves: In the cutting-with-planes strategy (day 3) mullion and transom curves lie in a plane. The profile can be produced by bending in this plane (or in faceted pieces).
  2. Space curves: In UV division the curves are usually not planar; the profile must be both bent and twisted, which is expensive. In practice the curve is divided into straight profile pieces.
  3. Profile orientation: At each point the profile's "up" is the surface normal. The profile rotating along the normal direction means the profile is twisted.

2. Nodes (1 hour)

Mullions and transoms meet at panel corners:

  1. Torsion-free node: If the centre planes of all members at a node intersect in a single axis, the node is simplified. This is possible with well-chosen offsets of planar quad meshes (day 3).
  2. Four members or six? 4 members in a quad mesh, 6 in a triangle mesh; a triangular node is expensive and heavy.
  3. Typed nodes: Node angles can also be grouped (the day 5 logic); if the node part is CNC-machined different angles are no problem, but if it is cast or moulded the number of types matters.

3. Connection and adjustment (1 hour)

  1. The substructure is connected to the building with adjustable brackets; on a curved façade bracket lengths and angles vary from point to point.
  2. Generate bracket points in Grasshopper: surface point, normal direction, distance to slab edge → bracket length.
  3. Group the bracket lengths (e.g. 6 types in 25 mm steps); the adjustment allowance takes the difference.
  4. Survey deviations (Façade Installation, day 2) are more critical on a curved façade; keep the adjustment allowance generous.

4. Unrolling cladding panels (30 min)

To get the flat cutting shape of a single-curved metal panel, unroll it with UnrollSrf in Rhino (for developable surfaces) or with plug-ins in Grasshopper. For edge folds in sheet bending, a bend allowance (from the manufacturer for the material and thickness) is added separately.

End-of-day check (15 min)

DAY 07

Fabrication data

~4 hours · 0/11 checks

Goal

On the last day you turn panelisation into fabrication data: coding, panel list, DXF output, checks and transferring the model into BIM. Then the final project. Time about 4 hours.

1. Coding (45 min)

Every panel must have an identity, and this identity must be the same in the drawing, Excel, fabrication, dispatch and installation:

P123 - CP - B - 07 - 14        project - facade type - zone - row - column
P123-CP-B-07-14 → type T-031

The location code (where?) and the type code (what?) are separate: 12 panels of the same type can be in 12 different locations.

2. Panel list (1 hour)

Column Source
Location code, type code Coding
Corner coordinates (4 × XYZ) Panel corners
Edge and diagonal lengths Signature (day 5)
Area, weight Surface area × unit weight
Deviation (mm) Planarity (day 2)
Status Flat / cold-bent / bent

To turn Grasshopper data trees into rows and write them to CSV, a Script component or Excel plug-ins are used. The list is the input to the take-off and purchasing in the Façade Quantity Take-off course.

3. DXF and labels (45 min)

  1. For each type, the panel's flat cutting shape (laid onto its own plane), with its label.
  2. In Rhino panel plane → XY plane with Orient; in Grasshopper the Orient component.
  3. Layer standard: cut lines, holes and labels on separate layers; units mm.

4. Checks (45 min)

5. Transfer to BIM (30 min)

Ways to hand the model over for coordination with panel identity and properties are on day 7 of the Navisworks and BIM Coordination course: Revit elements via Rhino.Inside.Revit, or IFC. Carrying the panel identity as a property (parameter) links the BIM model to the fabrication list.

6. Final project (15 min + your own time)

Choose a free surface (or build a 30 × 15 m "bump" surface) and:

  1. Divide it into panels with floor and grid planes.
  2. Produce the deviation map and classify the panels as flat / cold-bent / bent.
  3. Group them into types with 5 mm tolerance.
  4. Approximate the form with a translational surface and repeat the same analysis; compare the results.
  5. Export the panel list as CSV and the cutting shapes as DXF.

End-of-day check (15 min)
