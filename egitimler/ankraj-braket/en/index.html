Anchor and Bracket Design

A diagram-rich 7-day programme on how a curtain wall is connected to the building. You progress from the load path to fixed and sliding supports, from wind, dead load and earthquake to load combinations, from adjustment and tolerances to cast-in channel and anchor selection, from bracket plate and bolt calculation to movement and storey drift. At the end of the week you size the anchorage of one façade axis from start to finish.

7 days~4 hours a dayEnglishCode examples can be copied

Before you start

Who is it for

Engineers, architects and draughtspeople working, or preparing to work, in the design and structural teams of façade companies; site and technical office staff who check anchor and bracket drawings.

What you need

  • Structural basics: force, moment, stress; the Structural Basics course helps.
  • Introductory knowledge of façade systems; Facade Design Fundamentals.
  • A calculator or Excel; the Wind Load, Mullion Deflection and Bracket and Anchor Load tools on this site.

DAY 01

Load path and supports

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know the functions of a curtain wall anchorage, how load flows from glass to concrete, the difference between fixed and sliding supports, bracket types and why the anchorage must be adjustable in three directions. Time about 4 hours.

About this course: The calculations are for preliminary sizing and teaching. The examples follow a stick (mullion-transom) system: 1.5 m module, 3.6 m storey height, façade weight 60 kg/m², wind suction 1.6 kN/m². For the actual project the standards, the anchor manufacturer's ETA and the qualified engineer's structural calculation govern. The Bracket and Anchor Load tool on this site performs this course's calculations step by step.

1. Functions of the anchorage (45 min)

The anchorage (brackets, consoles, connections) is the set of parts connecting the façade to the building structure. It has four functions:

  1. Transfer loads: Carry the façade's dead load, wind and earthquake to the concrete.
  2. Allow movement: Accommodate thermal expansion, storey drift and building deflection without loading the façade.
  3. Provide adjustment: Take up building tolerances in three directions.
  4. Separate: Separate dissimilar metals (galvanic corrosion), heat (thermal bridge) and sound.

If one of these is forgotten, the façade either fails to carry load, cracks, or cannot be installed at all.

2. Load path (1 hour)

The load reaches the concrete by passing from each part of the façade to the next:

glass → gasket and pressure plate → transom → mullion → bracket → connection bolt → anchor (channel or anchor bolt) → concrete slab

Each link passes load to the next; the chain is only as strong as its weakest link. In design each link is checked separately:

Link Check
Mullion Deflection and stress (Mullion Deflection tool)
Mullion–bracket bolt Shear, bearing (day 5)
Bracket Bending, weld (day 5)
Anchor Tension, shear, concrete failure (days 4 and 5)

3. Fixed and sliding supports (1 hour)

A mullion is usually connected to the building with one bracket per floor and continues into the next mullion with a splice (spigot). Supports are of two kinds:

  • Fixed (dead load) support: Carries dead load and wind. The mullion is held vertically at this point.
  • Sliding (wind) support: Carries wind only; thanks to a slotted hole the mullion can slide freely vertically.

Why? An aluminium mullion expands with heat (about 5 mm over 3.6 m for a 60 °C difference) and floors move relative to each other. If the mullion were fixed vertically at two points this movement would be restrained, stressing the mullion or the bracket.

Common arrangement: the mullion is hung from the top (fixed support at the top, mullion in tension) and its lower end slides into the mullion below via the splice. A hung mullion has no buckling risk, which is why most systems use this arrangement.

4. Bracket types (45 min)

Type Where Note
Top of slab On top of the slab, near the edge Most common; stays within the screed, hidden
Slab edge On the outer (vertical) face of the slab When there is no screed or the slab edge is insufficient
Underside of slab On the underside of the slab Within the ceiling void; difficult access
Column / wall face On a vertical structural member In tall halls without slabs
Secondary steelwork On secondary steelwork For large gaps, when the façade is far from the slab

5. Adjustment in three directions (15 min)

A bracket must provide adjustment in three directions:

  • X (along the façade): Bring the mullion onto its grid line.
  • Y (perpendicular to the façade, in–out): Take up slab edge deviations.
  • Z (vertical): Bring the mullion to its level.

Adjustment ranges and how they are locked are the subject of day 3.

Exercise: Write the load path for a mullion in the façade of a building you know and state the material of each link. Show the positions of the fixed and sliding supports in a vertical section sketch.

DAY 02

Loads and combinations

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to calculate the dead, wind and earthquake loads on a bracket, distinguish characteristic and design values, build load combinations and know what storey drift does to the anchorage. Time about 4 hours.

1. Load area (30 min)

The area carried by a bracket is the half modules on both sides of the mullion times one storey height:

A = module × storey height = 1.5 m × 3.6 m = 5.40 m²

For corner mullions and mullions carrying canopies or sun shades, additional loads are added to this area.

2. Dead load (45 min)

Façade weight consists of glass, profiles, panels, insulation and accessories. Typical values:

Façade Weight (kg/m²)
Stick system, double glazing 45–60
Unitized, double glazing + spandrel 55–75
Laminated triple glazing 70–90
Stone cladding (ventilated) 70–100

Example: 60 kg/m² × 5.40 m² = 324 kg

Gk = 324 × 9.81 / 1000 = 3.18 kN (characteristic dead load)

To find glass weight quickly you can use the Glass Weight tool.

3. Wind (1 hour)

Wind load is calculated to EN 1991-1-4 (see the Wind Load tool). Two values matter for the façade:

  • Pressure: Pushes the façade towards the building. The bracket works in compression.
  • Suction: Pulls the façade outwards. It is largest in corner zones (zone A) and is often governing because it pulls the anchor out of the concrete.

Example: suction 1.6 kN/m² × 5.40 m² → Wk = 8.64 kN

This is almost three times the dead load. On tall buildings and at corners, wind is the main load in anchor design.

4. Earthquake and storey drift (45 min)

Seismic force: The façade is a non-structural building element; the seismic force is calculated with the rules for non-structural elements in the Turkish seismic code TBDY 2018 (comparable to EN 1998), based on the element's weight, its floor and the seismic hazard. For light façades this force is usually smaller than wind, but it can govern for heavy cladding (stone, GRC) and in high seismic zones.

Storey drift: In an earthquake two floors move horizontally relative to each other. The anchorage and the façade must accommodate this movement without failing. Example: a relative storey drift ratio of 1% means 36 mm horizontal movement over a 3.6 m storey. In stick systems this is taken by the mullion splice and the slotted holes of the bracket; in unitized systems by panels sliding relative to each other (day 6).

5. Characteristic value, design value and combinations (45 min)

In design, loads are multiplied by partial safety factors (EN 1990):

Load Factor (unfavourable)
Permanent (dead load) G 1,35
Variable (wind) W 1,50

Combination: Ed = 1.35 Gk + 1.5 Wk

  • Gd = 1.35 × 3.18 = 4.29 kN
  • Wd = 1.5 × 8.64 = 12.96 kN

Where wind suction pulls the anchorage out, the dead load may help in the opposite direction; then the favourable factor (1.0) is used for dead load. This is why a separate combination is built for each load direction. Deflection checks use unfactored (characteristic) loads.

Exercise: Calculate the bracket loads (Gk, Wk, Gd, Wd) for a corner mullion with a 1.8 m module, 4.2 m storey height, 70 kg/m² weight and 2.1 kN/m² suction. Compare the result with the Bracket and Anchor Load tool.

DAY 03

Adjustment and tolerance

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will know how much a reinforced concrete building can deviate, how to compare survey results with bracket adjustment ranges, how adjustment is provided and locked with slotted holes and serrated plates, and the adjustment sequence. Time about 4 hours.

1. Building tolerances (1 hour)

A reinforced concrete building is not built to millimetre accuracy. The slab edge may deviate in or out, the floor level up or down. Accepted deviations are given in the project specification and the relevant execution standards; in façade projects ±20–25 mm for the slab edge and ±10–20 mm for floor levels are common values.

The façade, on the other hand, is manufactured much more precisely (a few millimetres). The anchorage is the part that bridges the difference between these two worlds:

required adjustment range ≥ building tolerance + anchor placement tolerance + safety margin

Example: slab edge ±20 mm, anchor channel position ±10 mm → the bracket must provide at least ±30 mm Y adjustment.

2. Survey and decision (45 min)

The survey (site measurement) is done before fabrication: the slab edge and level are measured at each anchor point and the results entered in a deviation table (see Fabrication Drawings, day 6).

Deviation Decision
Within the adjustment range Standard bracket
Slightly outside the adjustment range Extended bracket or packing plate (special item)
Far outside Correction on the building side (breaking out, added concrete) or special steelwork; engineer's decision

3. How is adjustment provided? (1 h 15 min)

Direction Common solution
X (along the façade) Sliding the bolt along the anchor channel or a slotted hole in the bracket base
Y (in–out) Slotted hole in the bracket arm; threaded adjustment bolt
Z (vertical) Vertical slotted hole in the mullion or bracket; adjustment bolt or shims

Adjustment provided by slotted holes must not slip under load. Ways to prevent slip:

  • Serrated plate: The teeth of the two surfaces interlock; it relies on shape, not friction. Reliable for permanent directions such as wind and dead load.
  • Welding: The plate is welded once adjustment is complete. It damages galvanizing; cold galvanizing paint is needed afterwards.
  • Preloaded (friction-grip) bolt: A bolt tightened to a set torque holds by friction; torque control is essential.
  • Locking pin or tab after adjustment.

The slotted hole at the sliding support, however, is deliberately left free: the bolt is not tightened (or is left at a set torque with a slip washer and lock nut) so that thermal movement can occur.

4. Adjustment sequence (45 min)

  1. Floor levels and grid lines are marked on site (survey team).
  2. Brackets are loosely fitted to channels or anchors.
  3. The mullion is hung; level is set with Z, grid line with X, façade plane with Y.
  4. Check: façade plane and plumb with a laser.
  5. Fixed supports are locked (serrations, torque, weld), sliding supports left free.
  6. Inspection record: adjustment value and locking record for each bracket.

Exercise: Survey results for 8 mullions on one floor: +12, +18, −5, +27, −22, +8, +31, −14 mm (slab edge deviation). If the bracket Y adjustment is ±25 mm, which points need a special solution? Propose a solution for each from the decision table.

DAY 04

Cast-in channel and anchors

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to compare cast-in anchor channels with mechanical and chemical anchors, read an ETA, and know how edge distance, spacing, cracked concrete and failure modes affect anchor resistance. Time about 4 hours.

1. Anchor types (1 hour)

Type How Pros Cons
Cast-in anchor channel Fixed to the formwork before concrete is poured (Halfen-type C channel) No drilling of concrete; free adjustment in X; high resistance Position must be exact before pouring; hard to remedy if omitted
Mechanical (expansion) anchor A hole is drilled in hardened concrete; the anchor expands when tightened Fast, loadable immediately Stresses the concrete near edges; care in cracked concrete
Chemical (bonded) anchor Mortar into the hole, then the anchor rod Good near edges and for high loads Curing time; hole cleaning is critical
Undercut anchor An enlarged recess at the bottom of the hole Very high resistance, good in cracked concrete Expensive, special tools

In façade projects the main anchors are often cast-in channels; where channels are missing or misplaced, chemical or mechanical anchors are used instead.

2. The ETA (45 min)

Anchors and channels used for structural purposes must have a European Technical Assessment (ETA). The ETA gives:

  • Which concrete class and whether cracked/uncracked concrete the product may be used in,
  • Characteristic resistances and the design method (EN 1992-4),
  • Minimum edge distances, spacings and member thickness,
  • Installation torque, hole diameter and depth, curing times.

An anchor without an ETA, or used outside the ETA conditions, must not carry structural load.

In practice the calculation is done in the manufacturer's software (e.g. Hilti PROFIS Engineering, fischer FIXPERIENCE, Würth, Halfen HCW). The software takes the forces you will see in this course and calculates the anchor resistance.

3. Failure modes (1 hour)

An anchor loaded in tension can fail in one of these ways; the resistance is the smallest:

In tension In shear
Steel failure Steel failure
Pull-out (anchor slips out of the hole) Concrete edge failure (load towards an edge)
Concrete cone failure Concrete pry-out
Concrete splitting
Bond failure between mortar and concrete (chemical anchors)

Concrete cone failure often governs façade anchors: the anchor pulls a cone-shaped piece out of the concrete. The larger the cone, the higher the resistance; proximity to an edge or another anchor cuts the cone and reduces resistance.

4. Edge distance, spacing, cracked concrete (45 min)

  • Edge distance (c): Distance from the anchor to the concrete edge. Anchors in the slab edge are by nature close to the edge; this is the main weakness of façade anchorages. If the wind shear acts towards the edge, concrete edge failure governs.
  • Spacing (s): Distance between two anchors. The cones of anchors that are too close overlap; the total resistance does not increase in proportion to the number of anchors.
  • Cracked concrete: The tension zone of the slab (often the edges) is assumed cracked. Resistance drops markedly in cracked concrete; façade anchors are usually designed assuming cracked concrete.
  • Concrete class and thickness: A higher class and a thicker slab increase resistance.

Practical rule: Instead of an anchor very close to the edge, consider a cast-in channel, deeper embedment, a longer arm or top-of-slab fixing.

Exercise: Open an anchor's ETA (you can download it from the manufacturer's website); find the minimum edge distance, installation torque and characteristic tension resistance in cracked concrete.

DAY 05

Anchor and bracket calculation

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to distribute bracket loads to anchors as tension and shear, check tension–shear interaction, and carry out the bending check of the bracket plate and fillet weld and bolt checks at preliminary sizing level. Time about 4 hours.

1. Anchor forces: slab-edge bracket (1 h 15 min)

The bracket is fixed to the outer face of the slab with two anchors; the upper anchor is in tension, the lower end of the bracket bears on the concrete. The dead load acts e = 120 mm from the concrete, the internal lever arm is z = 120 mm.

With yesterday's values: Gd = 4.29 kN, Wd = 12.96 kN (suction), n = 2 anchors, anchors in tension nt = 1.

  • Shear (dead load): V = Gd / n = 4.29 / 2 = 2.15 kN
  • Tension (suction + moment of dead load): N = Wd / n + Gd · e / (z · nt) = 12.96 / 2 + 4.29 × 120 / (120 × 1) = 6.48 + 4.29 = 10.77 kN

If the eccentricity e doubles, the tension from the moment doubles too: keeping the bracket close to the concrete makes the anchor smaller.

2. Anchor forces: top-of-slab bracket (30 min)

The bracket sits on top of the slab with vertical anchors. This time wind shears the anchor, dead load goes into the concrete in compression, but the eccentricity of the dead load and the height of the wind force (hb) cause tension:

  • V = Wd / n
  • N = (Gd · e + Wd · hb) / (z · nt)

With the same loads and hb = 80 mm: V = 6.48 kN, N = (4.29 × 120 + 12.96 × 80) / 120 = 12.93 kN.

3. Interaction check (30 min)

If the anchor is in both tension and shear, their combined effect is checked (EN 1992-4):

βN = N / NRd ≤ 1, βV = V / VRd ≤ 1 and βN1.5 + βV1.5 ≤ 1

Example: if the manufacturer's software gives NRd = 15 kN and VRd = 12 kN (cracked concrete, edge distance included), for the slab-edge bracket:

βN = 10.77 / 15 = 0.72 · βV = 2.15 / 12 = 0.18 · 0.721.5 + 0.181.5 = 0.61 + 0.08 = 0.69 ≤ 1 ✓

You can follow this calculation step by step in the Bracket and Anchor Load tool.

4. Bracket plate (45 min)

The section of the bracket arm at the concrete face carries the moment M = Gd · e:

M = 4.29 kN × 120 mm = 515 kN·mm

The section modulus of a rectangular plate is W = b · t² / 6. With plate width b = 100 mm:

t (mm) W (mm³) σ = M / W (MPa) S235 (235 MPa)
8 1067 483 ✕
10 1667 309 ✕
12 2400 215 ✓

A 12 mm plate looks sufficient. Real design considers the full load combination, hole weakening, plate torsion and the weld; this table only shows the order of magnitude.

5. Weld and bolts (45 min)

  • Fillet weld: The throat thickness (a) is usually chosen as 0.5–0.7 times the thinnest plate joined; weld length and throat are checked to EN 1993-1-8 to resist the force on the weld. Avoid site welding: quality control is difficult and it damages galvanizing.
  • Mullion–bracket bolt: Stainless A4-70 or galvanized 8.8 bolts. Checks: bolt shear, bearing on the aluminium mullion wall (aluminium is softer than steel, bearing often governs) and edge distance. If the aluminium wall is thin, a sleeve (bush) or reinforcing plate is used.

Exercise: If e were 180 mm, what would N, the interaction and the required thickness of a 100 mm wide plate be for the slab-edge bracket? Calculate with the tool and by hand and compare.

DAY 06

Material, corrosion and movement

~4 hours · 0/5 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will be able to choose bracket materials and corrosion protection, know how to prevent galvanic corrosion, calculate how the mullion splice accommodates thermal movement and storey drift, and know the logic of unitized panel connections. Time about 4 hours.

1. Material and corrosion protection (1 hour)

Material Use Protection
Structural steel S235 / S355 Brackets, welded consoles Hot-dip galvanizing (EN ISO 1461)
Stainless steel (A2/304, A4/316) Bolts, washers, brackets at the seaside Its own passive layer; A4 in marine and chloride environments
Aluminium (6060, 6063, 6082) Extruded brackets, mullions Anodizing or paint; durable by itself in most environments

The bracket often stays inside the screed or ceiling void and is never seen during its life. The corrosivity class (C2…C5) is chosen by environment; damp screeds and seaside locations need heavier classes.

2. Galvanic corrosion (45 min)

If two different metals touch in the presence of moisture, the "less noble" one corrodes. The most common pair in façades: aluminium mullion + steel or stainless bracket. Here it is the aluminium that corrodes.

Measures:

  • Separator: A plastic (PA, PE) or EPDM separating plate at the contact surface.
  • At the bolt: Insulating sleeve and washer; the stainless bolt must not touch the aluminium directly.
  • Keep it dry: A detail that does not trap water, drainage.
  • Area ratio: A small stainless bolt on a large aluminium surface is relatively harmless; a large stainless plate on a small aluminium part is dangerous.

The separator plate also reduces the thermal bridge and prevents friction noise (creaking).

3. Mullion splice and thermal movement (1 hour)

With one mullion per floor, there is a joint between mullions with a splice (spigot) inside. The splice fits tightly in the lower mullion and loosely in the upper one, keeping both mullions in line while allowing vertical movement.

The joint gap must accommodate these movements:

  1. Thermal movement: ΔL = α · L · ΔT. Aluminium (α = 23·10⁻⁶), L = 3600 mm, ΔT = 60 K → 5.0 mm.
  2. Slab deflection and creep: If the slab above deflects downwards, the hung mullion moves down too (value from the structural design).
  3. Installation tolerance.

Example: 5 mm thermal + 8 mm slab deflection + 2 mm tolerance → a joint of at least 15 mm; the gasketed joint profile must cover this width. You can calculate thermal movement quickly with the Thermal Expansion tool.

4. Storey drift (45 min)

In an earthquake floors move horizontally (day 2: 1% drift → 36 mm over a 3.6 m storey). In a stick system this movement is accommodated by:

  • Bending of the mullion and rotation at the splice,
  • The horizontal clearance of the slotted holes at the sliding support,
  • Sufficient edge clearance so the glass can rotate within its frame.

If the glass edge clearance is insufficient, the glass corner touches the profile and breaks. That is why edge clearances and setting block layout are checked against drift in seismic zones.

5. Unitized panel connections (30 min)

A unitized panel is a module finished in the factory and is hung on brackets on site:

  • At the top of the panel a hook profile sits in the bracket on the slab.
  • Adjustment is provided by adjustment bolts on the bracket (level) and the bracket's slotted holes (X, Y).
  • Panels join side by side and one above the other with male–female (stack joint) profiles; thermal movement and drift are taken up by sliding in these joints.

In a unitized system the anchorage sets the installation speed: brackets must be in the right place in advance so that each panel can be hung from the crane in one go.

Exercise: Calculate the thermal movement of a dark (ΔT = 70 K) aluminium mullion with a 4.2 m storey height. If slab deflection is 10 mm and tolerance 3 mm, how wide must the splice joint be at least?

DAY 07

Project: anchoring one axis

~4 hours · 0/4 checks

Goal

By the end of the day you will have sized the anchorage of one curtain wall axis step by step from load to installation, and learned what an anchor drawing must contain and the checklist. Time about 4 hours.

1. Project data (15 min)

A 12-storey office building in Istanbul, stick-system curtain wall:

Data Value
Module 1.5 m
Storey height 3.6 m
Façade weight 60 kg/m²
Wind suction (corner, top floor) 1.6 kN/m²
Bracket To the slab edge, 2 anchors, e = 120 mm, z = 120 mm
Concrete C30/37, assumed cracked
Mullion Aluminium, Ix = 400 cm⁴, Wx = 36 cm³

2. Step-by-step design (2 h 30 min)

Step 1 — Mullion: Enter L = 3600 mm, module 1500 mm, w = 1.2 kN/m² (middle zone pressure) in the Mullion Deflection tool. Are deflection and stress acceptable? Repeat with 1.6 kN/m² for the corner zone; could corner mullions be a separate item?

Step 2 — Loads: Gk = 3.18 kN, Wk = 8.64 kN; Gd = 4.29 kN, Wd = 12.96 kN (day 2).

Step 3 — Support arrangement: Fixed support at the top of each floor, sliding support at the bottom, splice joint 15 mm (day 6). The sliding support takes wind only.

Step 4 — Anchor forces: N = 10.77 kN, V = 2.15 kN (day 5). Calculate N and V yourself for the bracket at the sliding support (no dead load).

Step 5 — Anchor selection: Enter the forces and geometry (edge distance, member thickness, cracked concrete) in the manufacturer's software; get NRd, VRd and the interaction. In the example 0.69 ≤ 1 ✓.

Step 6 — Bracket: Plate 100 × 12 mm S235, hot-dip galvanized (day 5). Mullion–bracket connection 2 × M10 A4-70 bolts, bearing check on the aluminium wall, EPDM separator plate.

Step 7 — Adjustment: Expected survey: slab edge ±20 mm, channel position ±10 mm → bracket Y adjustment ±30 mm; X along the channel; Z 30 mm slotted hole and serrated plate (day 3).

Step 8 — Earthquake and drift: Get the storey drift value from the project structural engineer; check that the slotted holes at the sliding support and the glass edge clearances accommodate this movement.

3. Anchor drawing (45 min)

The anchor design is transferred to the drawing with this information:

  • Anchor layout plan: channel or anchor positions dimensioned from the grid lines, edge distances, levels,
  • Bracket detail: material, thickness, holes, slot sizes, welds, surface protection,
  • Connection detail: bolt grade and torque, separator plate, washer and nut,
  • Anchor data: product and ETA number, hole diameter and depth, installation torque, curing time,
  • Adjustment ranges and locking method,
  • General notes: concrete class, cracked concrete assumption, post-installation testing (percentage of pull-out tests).

For the drawing side, see the Fabrication Drawings course.

4. Checklist (15 min)

Check ✓
Load area, dead load and wind zones (corners!) correct
A combination built for each load direction (suction and pressure)
Fixed and sliding supports distinguished in the detail
Anchor within ETA conditions: concrete class, edge distance, cracking
Interaction ≤ 1, bracket plate and weld sufficient
Galvanic separation and corrosion protection specified
Adjustment range ≥ building tolerance + placement tolerance
Splice joint covers thermal + deflection + tolerance
Storey drift checked

Next steps: Install an anchor manufacturer's free calculation software and calculate this project with real products. Study the façade application guides of anchor channel manufacturers. To deepen the structural side, continue with the Structural Basics and Introduction to Finite Elements courses.
